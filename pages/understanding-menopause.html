Understanding Menopause

Menopause and perimenopause are natural stages of life, but for many people they arrive with confusion, worry and very little reliable information. This page is here to change that.

What Is Menopause?

Menopause is the point in time when someone has gone 12 consecutive months without a menstrual period. It marks the end of the reproductive years and usually happens between the ages of 45 and 55, although it can occur earlier or later.

Menopause itself is just one moment on a longer journey. The years leading up to it—when hormones begin to fluctuate and symptoms appear—are called perimenopause. The years after are known as postmenopause. Understanding all three stages helps make sense of what’s happening in the body and why symptoms can change over time.

Perimenopause: The Transition Phase

Perimenopause is the transitional period before menopause. During this time, the ovaries gradually produce less oestrogen and progesterone. Hormone levels can rise and fall like a rollercoaster, which is why symptoms can be unpredictable—better one month, harder the next.

Perimenopause can last several years. Periods may become irregular, lighter or heavier, or occur closer together or further apart. It’s still possible to become pregnant during this phase, so contraception is usually recommended until at least one year after the final period if over 50, or two years if under 50.

Common Symptoms

Not everyone experiences menopause in the same way. Some people have very few symptoms; others find that symptoms affect sleep, mood, relationships, work and confidence. All of these experiences are valid.

Symptoms can begin in perimenopause, continue through menopause and sometimes persist into postmenopause:

  • Changes to periods (timing, flow, frequency)
  • Hot flushes and night sweats
  • Sleep problems and fatigue
  • Mood changes, anxiety, low mood or irritability
  • Brain fog, memory and concentration difficulties
  • Vaginal dryness or discomfort during sex
  • More frequent urinary tract infections
  • Joint and muscle aches
  • Changes in weight and body shape
  • Reduced sex drive
  • Skin and hair changes

These symptoms are linked to changing hormone levels, particularly falling oestrogen. They can be mild or severe, short‑lived or long‑lasting. If symptoms are affecting daily life, relationships or work, it’s worth speaking to a healthcare professional—there are options that can help.

Why Understanding Menopause Matters

Menopause is not a disease—it’s a normal stage of life. But the transition can affect almost every part of wellbeing: physical health, emotional balance, sleep, sexual health, bone strength and heart health. Knowing what’s happening and why can replace fear with understanding and help people make informed choices about lifestyle changes, treatments and support.

Understanding menopause also matters for partners, families, friends and workplaces. When everyone has better information, it becomes easier to talk openly, offer support and create environments where people don’t have to struggle in silence.

What Can Help?

There is no single “right” way to navigate menopause. For some, simple changes—such as adjusting diet, exercise, sleep routines, stress management and reducing alcohol or smoking—make a big difference. Others benefit from treatments like hormone therapy, non‑hormonal medicines or vaginal oestrogen, depending on symptoms and medical history.

Good care is personalised. A GP, menopause specialist or nurse can help explore options, weigh risks and benefits, and find an approach that fits someone’s health, values and preferences.
